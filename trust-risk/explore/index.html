You calculate. Just not the way you think

In 1979, Kahneman and Tversky asked people to choose between bets, then reverse-engineered the maths people were using. We do calculate expected value, just not with the real numbers: we overweight small probabilities and feel losses more than gains.

This work won the Nobel Prize in 2002. Now I'm testing if the same maths explains trust.

Sensitivity to outcomes

How we perceive gains and losses. Losses loom larger than gains (loss aversion), and marginal value decreases as amounts grow. So people are risk-averse for gains but risk-seeking for losses.

Value function v(x)
  • α0.88
  • λ2.25
  • Perfect rationality
α 0.88

How much you care about the amount versus simply winning or losing. Lower values = the amount matters less.

λ 2.25

λ = 2.25 means a loss weighs 2.25 times more than a gain of the same size. Higher values = losses hurt more.

How we weight probabilities

We don't perceive probabilities linearly. Small chances feel bigger than they are, while medium and high probabilities feel less certain.

Weighting function w(p)
  • γ+0.61
  • γ−0.69
  • Perfect rationality
γ+ 0.61

1 means probabilities are taken as they are. The further from 1, the stronger the weighting.

γ− 0.69

Same concept as gains, but for losses. Typically similar to γ+ but empirically slightly different.

Real-world examples

  • –Lottery tickets: we overweight the tiny chance of winning millions
  • –Insurance: we overweight small disaster risks
